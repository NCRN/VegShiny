Introduction

The National Park Service preserves unimpaired the natural and cultural resources and values of the National Park System. The National Capital Region Inventory and Monitoring Network (NCRN) is part of the National Park Service Inventory and Monitoring Division, which conducts long-term, ecological monitoring in national parks. The information we provide helps park managers make sound, science-based management decisions that help the National Park Service meet its mission to conserve America's most treasured places for the benefit of future generations. NCRN gathers information on forest vegetation and other ecological vital signs in the national parks of the National Capital Region, which includes parts of Virginia, West Virginia, Maryland, and the District of Columbia.

Sampling locations

As of January 2026, NCRN monitors forest vegetation at 427 plots in eleven National Parks in the National Capital Region. NCRN has added and removed plots since 2006. For details on changes to sampling sites through time, see page 5 in NCRN's Long-Term Forest Vegetation Monitoring Protocol.

Sampling frequency

NCRN began monitoring forest vegetation in 2006 and monitoring is ongoing. The 427 plots monitored are divided into four roughly equal panels, with each panel monitored once every four years. For details on the sampling frequency methods, see page 7 in NCRN's Long-Term Forest Vegetation Monitoring Protocol.

  • From 2006 to 2009, NCRN monitored 400 plots, divided into four panels of 100 plots, with each panel sampled once every four years.
  • From 2010-2013, after the first sampling cycle (2006-2009), additional sample plots were added in the smaller NCRN parks (ANTI, MONO, and WOTR), bringing the total to 427 active plots

    • Parameters

    As of January 2026, NCRN monitors 6 primary vegetation types (listed below). A more detailed description of monitored vegetation is available in the NCRN forest vegetation monitoring protocol.

    1. Trees
      2. Trees are defined as woody plants that have a diameter at breast height (DBH) - diameter taken at a height of 1.37 meters or 4.5 feet - of at least 10 centimeters (~4 inches). Trees are identified to species, their DBH is measured, and plant health assessments are conducted, including: position in the canopy; presence of injuries to the bark, branches, or large numbers of leaves; presence of diseases or insect pests; and the presence of vines growing on the trees. DBH is used to calculate the 'basal area' of the tree (a cross section of the trunk), which is used as a measure of the tree size.
    2. Saplings
      3. Saplings are small trees with a DBH between 1 and 10 centimeters (0.4 - 4 inches). Like trees, saplings are identified and their diameter is measured. In addition to the plant health observations for trees, NCRN assesses saplings to see if their leaves have been browsed by deer.
    3. Shrubs
      4. Shrubs are woody plant species that typically divide into multiple stems at the base of the plants where they emerge from the ground. This differentiates them from trees, which are typically a single stem at the base. Shrubs are identified to species and assessed for plant health and deer browse.
    4. Tree and Shrub Seedlings
      5. Seedlings are trees or shrubs with a DBH of less than 1 centimeter. Data is only collected on seedlings that are at least 15 centimeters (~ 6 inches) tall. Smaller seedlings are not recorded. NCRN identifies seedlings to species, measures their height, and looks for evidence of deer browse.
    5. Understory Plants and Herbaceous Plants
      6. Understory and herbaceous plants are a broad category which includes a variety of species found on the forest floor. Only a subset of understory plant species is monitored, primarily species which are either exotic invasives or are known to be preferentially consumed by deer. Most are not woody, but a few are woody shrubs that either grow as numerous small individuals (e.g. blueberries) or are found in dense thorny thickets (e.g. Japanese barberry). Find a list of plant species monitored as understory plants in Table 14 of the NCRN forest vegetation monitoring protocol has a list of herbaceous plant species monitored as understory plants. Note: A plant can be monitored both as an understory plant and as a vine when it occurs both on trees and on the forest floor (e.g. English ivy). NCRN identifies each plant to species and estimates its percent cover (this is done by examining a number of 1-meter squared quadrats and determining the percent of the ground covered by that species).
    6. Vines in Trees
      7. Every tree in a monitoring plot is inspected for the presence of vines. The species of each vine is recorded. This website provides information on how many trees are hosts to vines. The abundance of each vine species is estimated by the number of trees they grow on. No attempt is made to measure the size of the individual vines.

Forestry Importance Value

Forestry Importance Values (IVs) are used to estimate the dominance of particular tree species in a forested area relative to other tree species. The values are calculated using data collected from monitoring plots and provide a general indication of which species are most dominant in a forest. This index was developed by Curtis and Macintosh (1951) to investigate variation in forest communities. No index can fully represent every aspect of ecological importance, but in practice IVs provide a general indication of species dominance in forests.

The Importance Value is made up of three components. Each component has a value between 0 and 1, and the IV is the sum of the three components.

Every species found has an importance value. You can see a graph of importance values of up to 20 species by clicking on "Graph", and you can view a table with all the selected species values by clicking on "Data table".

The three components of importance value are:

  • Relative Density:

    Density is typically measured as the number of individual plants (trees, saplings etc.) found per plot. Relative density (also known as relative abundance) measures the abundance of each species compared to the combined abundance of all species. For a given species "X", the relative density is:

    Relative density of X = (Density of X) / (Density of all Species)

  • Relative Dominance (Size)

    Relative dominance is a measure of the size of each species compared to the combined size of all species. Dominance of trees and saplings is measured as the basal area of the plant, which is the area of a cross-section of the plant measured at 1.37m above the ground. For seedlings, dominance is measured by the height of the seedlings. For a given species "X", the relative dominance is:

    Relative dominance of X = (Dominance of X) / (Dominance of all Species)

  • Relative Distribution

    Distribution is measured as the number of monitoring plots that a species occurs in. Relative distribution is a measure of how often each species is found compared to how often all species are found. For a given species "X", the relative distribution is:

    Relative distribution of X = (Distribution of X) / (Distribution of all Species)

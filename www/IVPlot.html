Importance Values

Importance Values (IVs) give a general indication of which species are the most dominant in a forest relative to other tree species. The index was developed by Curtis and McIntosh (1951) to investigate variation in forest communities. The Importance Value is made up of three components (below). Each component has a value between 0 and 1, and the IV is the sum of the three components.

Statistical Details: IV is made up of three components. Each component has a value between 0 and 1 ( IV is the sum of these components):

  • Relative Density: Density is typically measured as the number of individual plants (trees, saplings, etc.) found per plot. Relative density (also known as relative abundance) measures the abundance of each species compared to the combined abundance of all species. For a given species "X", the relative density is:

    Relative density of X = (Density of X) / (Density of all species)

  • Size (Relative Dominance): Relative dominance is a measure of the size of each species compared to the combined size of all species. Dominance of trees and saplings is measured as the basal area of the plant, which is the area of a cross-section of the plant measured at 1.37 meters above the ground. For seedlings, dominance is measured by the height of the seedlings. For a given species "X", the relative dominance is:

    Relative dominance of X = (Dominance of X) / (Dominance of all species)

  • Relative Distribution: Distribution is measured as the number of monitoring plots that a species occurs in. Relative distribution is a measure of how often each species is found compared to how often all species are found. For a given species "X", the relative distribution is:

    Relative distribution of X = (Distribution of X) / (Distribution of all species)
